Avoid high-intensity intervals when sharp pain, illness, or poor recovery is reported.

## progressive_overload_basics
Source: NSCA — Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning
URL: (paywalled — verify access)
Use: Plan generation, weekly progression

Progressive overload means gradually increasing training stress so the body keeps adapting.
For beginners, small consistent increments (added weight, an extra rep, or an extra set) work
better than large infrequent jumps. If weight cannot increase without breaking form, add a rep
or an extra set at the same weight before adding load.

## progressive_overload_plateau
Source: NSCA — Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning
URL: (paywalled — verify access)
Use: Plan repair, weekly summary

A plateau is generally defined as two or more sessions with no increase in reps, weight, or sets
at the same perceived effort. A plateau alone does not always mean more volume is needed — check
readiness and recovery trend first, since fatigue is a common hidden cause.

## deload_principles
Source: Periodization and recovery research (Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research)
URL: (paywalled — verify access)
Use: Plan repair, readiness-triggered recovery

A deload is a planned, short-term reduction in training volume or intensity, typically
one week, used to allow recovery before continuing progression. A single poor check-in can
justify a lighter or reduced session, while a true deload is more appropriate when readiness
trends downward over multiple consecutive check-ins. Cutting volume without a clear trigger
can reduce progress unnecessarily.

## recovery_sleep_readiness
Source: General sleep and exercise recovery guidance
URL: https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html
Use: Readiness scoring, plan repair

Sleep duration and quality strongly influence next-day training capacity and injury risk.
Consistently short sleep (under roughly 6 hours) is associated with reduced strength
performance and slower recovery. A single poor night may justify a modest intensity or
volume reduction, while a multi-day downward trend is the stronger signal for larger plan
repair.

## beginner_training_safety
Source: ACSM Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription
URL: https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/index.html
Use: Plan generation for new users

Beginners benefit from lower initial volume and a focus on movement quality before adding
load or intensity. Full-body sessions two to three times per week with adequate rest between
are generally appropriate starting points. New or unfamiliar exercises should be introduced one
at a time so form can be assessed before progression.

## protein_target_guidance
Source: International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) Position Stand
URL: https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/
Use: Nutrition target calculation

Daily protein intake of roughly 1.4–2.0 g per kg of body weight is generally sufficient for
building or maintaining muscle in most exercising individuals. During a calorie deficit,
higher intakes (roughly 2.0–2.4 g/kg) help preserve lean mass. Spreading protein across
multiple meals is generally favored over one large serving.

## calorie_deficit_safety
Source: General sports nutrition guidance
URL: (verify source before citing publicly)
Use: Nutrition target calculation, safety floor

A moderate deficit (roughly 15–20% below maintenance calories) supports fat loss while
limiting muscle loss and fatigue risk. Deficits significantly larger than this, or calorie
targets below general safe minimums, increase risk of fatigue, poor recovery, and disordered
eating patterns, and should not be recommended without professional guidance.

## joint_specific_pain_gate
Source: General injury-safety guidance (non-diagnostic)
URL: (no single source — internal safety rule)
Use: Plan repair, safety gating

Pain that is sharp, worsening, severe, persistent, or clearly aggravated by loading a joint
should stop loading of that movement pattern rather than be pushed through. This guidance is
a general safety heuristic, not a diagnosis — persistent or worsening pain should be referred
to a qualified professional rather than resolved through plan adjustment alone.

## substitution_general_rules
Source: NSCA exercise technique guidance
URL: (paywalled — verify access)
Use: Plan repair, exercise substitution

When substituting an exercise due to pain, discomfort, or equipment limits, prefer a
replacement that trains the same primary muscle group through a similar movement pattern
(e.g. hinge, push, pull, squat) while reducing the specific range of motion or load that
caused the issue. Avoid substitutions that simply reduce effort without preserving the
training stimulus, unless the goal is an explicit recovery/deload session.

## substitution_equipment_limited
Source: General strength training guidance
URL: (no single source — internal compiled rule)
Use: Plan generation and repair for limited-equipment users

For users without access to barbells or machines, bodyweight and dumbbell variations can
train the same movement patterns by adjusting tempo, added pause reps, or reduced rest instead
of added external load. Progression for equipment-limited users should rely more heavily on
reps, tempo, and volume than on load increases.
